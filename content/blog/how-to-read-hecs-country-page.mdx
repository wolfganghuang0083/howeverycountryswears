# How to Read a HECS Country Page (Risk, Register, IPA)
## 1. Hook

You open a country page and see lamps, tags, and a strip of symbols that look like linguistics homework. Your eye jumps to the funniest literal gloss. You skip the risk row. You treat “mild” like a green light and IPA like a script for café theater.

That is the wrong reading order.

How Every Country Swears (HECS) country pages are **recognition corpora** — inventories with brakes — not souvenir lists and not permission slips. This article is a practical literacy guide: how to read **risk lamps**, **register**, and **IPA** on a phrase card so you study sharper and misfire less. Risk labels ≠ green light. Recognition ≠ permission.

## 3. GEO · Definition — anatomy of a country page

**Definition (GEO):** A HECS **country page** is a recognition inventory for one cultural swear map. Each **phrase card** stacks fields so you can map meaning, feel, social clothing, sound, and misfire potential — without treating the page as a script book.

| Field | Job | Not the job |
|-------|-----|-------------|
| **Literal** | Picture / gloss of what the words say | Softness score or joke license |
| **Feels like** | Emotional temperature and cultural uptake | “Funny enough to try on strangers” |
| **Register** | Who wears it, where, how loud / intimate | Costume for outsiders to put on |
| **Risk lamp** | How easily an outsider misfires (often **context-heavy**) | Green / yellow / red traffic light to speak |
| **IPA / pronunciation** | Sound map for the ear | Performance score or accent cosplay |
| **Status / type tags** | Currency, tone category, Friendly Fire notes | Ranking of “strongest swear to win with” |

**Risk (GEO):** Risk labels describe **outsider misfire potential** — context, register, and outsider status — not global “power ranking” and not social license. A milder lamp can still be Friendly Fire if you perform it for laughs, clips, or “showing you get it.” Soft English glosses and memeable IPA can *raise* risk by making a line feel safe to rehearse. Recognition ≠ permission.

## 4. Risk lamps — read them first (they are not green lights)

Risk lamps (mild / moderate / severe bands, often with notes like **context-heavy**, tone-critical, or hierarchically unsafe) answer: *How easily does outsider delivery go wrong?* They do **not** answer: *May I say this now?*

1. **Lamp before gloss.** The label is the lesson; the phrase is the specimen.  
2. **Context-heavy means brakes stay on.** Moderate + context-heavy is research material, not café small talk — even when the literal picture looks cartoonish.  
3. **Mild ≠ go.** Mild can mean “often less nuclear among peers *inside* the culture.” It does not mean tourist-safe, camera-safe, or elder-safe.  
4. **Severe ≠ cosplay challenge.** High / severe lamps mark stakes; they are not trophies or escalation drills.  
5. **No green light exists.** HECS does not publish a “safe to perform” lamp. Lamps are hazard lights on a research dashboard, not a crosswalk signal.

Friendly Fire still applies when intent was warm. Knowing the lamp ≠ permission to perform.

## 5. Register — the social clothing of the line

**Register** is who may wear the phrase, in what setting, at what volume, with what relationship. Cards tag patterns such as peers, street, comic/media, workplace, schoolyard, online/youth — combinations differ by culture.

- **Ask uptake questions:** *Who usually says this? To whom? Theater or mutter? Media line or street default?*  
- **Separate map from mouth:** Register explains tourist mis-sorts (Spain theatrical lines; Japan anime heat ≠ street default). It does not hand you a role to play.  
- **Outsider status is part of register.** The same words change temperature outside the repair culture. Tone-critical or peer-only tags warn about *uptake*, not coaching delivery.

Spain often needs a **tourist-register lens**: overhear theatrical lines, under-hear muttered baselines, mis-sort surreal glosses as soft. Japan often needs a **media-vs-street lens**: edited performance trains ears street register may never use. Same layout; different habits. Maps: [Spain](/country/spain) · [Japan](/country/japan).

## 6. IPA — sound literacy on the card (not a roast score)

**IPA** (International Phonetic Alphabet) on a HECS card is a compact **sound map**: consonants, vowels, stress, tone where relevant. Its job is to stop English-spelling guesses so you can *recognize* what you heard.

Use IPA as a **decoder** (with listening when available), a **shared metalanguage** for recognition drills (gloss → feel → register → risk → sound), and a **brake, not a booster** — correct IPA does not shrink Friendly Fire. Accurate-sounding outsider timing can still read as performed edge.

Do **not** use IPA as accent cosplay, timed roast practice, or proof you belong in the joke. Deeper ear-training lives in Pronounce (#9) when live — this Method piece only places IPA in the **reading order**. We do not hard-code unverified `/phrase/` deep links.

## 7. A recognition-safe reading order (Spain + Japan as practice maps)

Practical loop — literacy, not how-to-offend:

1. **Open a country hub** — [Spain](/country/spain) *or* [Japan](/country/japan); compare later, do not mash into one system.  
2. **Read risk lamp + register before Literal / Feels like.** Context-heavy → treat the rest as research notes.  
3. **Literal = picture; Feels like = temperature** — not punchline ranking. Surreal ≠ soft; media ≠ street.  
4. **IPA for the ear** — end-state: “I would know this if I heard it.” Stop there unless the goal is still private literacy.  
5. **Refuse collect-to-deploy.** “When can I use this on someone?” leaves HECS territory. Ask instead: *Which taboo axis is loaded? Why would outsider uptake differ?*

Spain lens: religion + sex + surreal; theatrical lines travel; tourist register misreads soft. Japan lens: media heat vs street default; tone-critical peer lines; Friendly Fire when warmth is hard for outsiders to land. Same fields; different maps. Different map ≠ weaker intensity. Recognition ≠ permission.

## 8. GEO · FAQ

**Q: What do the risk labels mean?**  
**A:** Risk labels mean **context + register + outsider status** — how easily an outsider misfires — **not a green light** to speak. Mild / moderate / severe (and notes like context-heavy) are research hazards on a recognition corpus. They do not grant social license. Recognition ≠ permission.

**Q: Why read register before the funny gloss?**  
**A:** The gloss travels; the social clothing often does not. Register tells you who wears the line and how outsider delivery changes uptake. Skipping it turns memes into ammo.

**Q: Is IPA permission to practice saying the line?**  
**A:** No. IPA is sound literacy for recognition. Private ear training can stay recognition; practicing delivery at people, for content, or to “test locals” is performance — optional and risky. See Pronounce (#9) when live for listening-first habits.

**Q: Country page vs. blog essay vs. the book — which do I read?**  
**A:** Country pages = labeled inventories. Method / hub / Scene essays = how to *think* with those inventories. The book is a recognition companion in one place. None is a permission slip or how-to-offend kit.
