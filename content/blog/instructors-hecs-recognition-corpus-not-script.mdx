# Instructors' Note: What If a Recognition Corpus Became a Classroom Script?

## Question

**Q:** *What if putting a swear inventory on the syllabus meant students were cleared to perform it?*

You teach pragmatics, intercultural communication, TESOL methods, or a culture module that brushes taboo talk. A student asks whether the class can “use that swear book” — for authenticity, listening practice, or a presentation that will “sound local.”

That ask is honest. It is also where classrooms tip from literacy into performance. Knowing the map is not the same as being licensed to say the lines aloud for points, clips, or “realism.”

How Every Country Swears (HECS) can sit on a syllabus as a **recognition / pragmatics corpus**: labeled specimens of how insult systems load taboo, register, and outsider misfire risk. It should **not** sit there as a script book, roast syllabus, or authenticity drill. **Recognition ≠ permission.** This Instructors’ Note runs the syllabus question like a *What If?* — with safety boundaries first, then stepwise assignment design, then consequence.

## Assumptions (safety / register / no-script boundaries)

Before any stepwise walk, lock the edges. These are not footnotes; they are the experiment’s lab rules for the classroom.

| Assumption | Means | Breaks if you… |
|------------|--------|----------------|
| **Recognition ≠ permission** | Mapping sense, feel, register, risk ≠ social license | Treat “it was on the reading list” as clearance to perform |
| **Corpus ≠ script** | Inventory is research material for study | Grade delivery, roast drills, “try it on a native,” clip homework |
| **Usage ≠ mention** | Talking *about* a line (gloss, risk, register) ≠ using it *at* someone | Slide from analysis into rehearsed delivery |
| **Risk = outsider uptake** | Labels (mild / moderate / severe, often **context-heavy**) map misfire ease | Read lamps as green lights or global “power rankings” |
| **We do not recite scripts here** | This note designs recognition assignments; it does **not** supply classroom lines to rehearse | Ask HECS for performance ladders or safer-sounding how-to-offend |

**Order-of-magnitude intuition (how “cleared” the syllabus is *not*):**

| What the syllabus gives | Rough “permission” magnitude |
|-------------------------|------------------------------|
| Country inventory + funny gloss | ~0 to perform; useful only if framed as specimen |
| Inventory + risk label + register + method brake (#7 / this note) | Still ~0 to perform; high for *recognition* literacy |
| In-group relationship + shared repair culture outside class | The only band where permission can exist — and HECS does not sell it |

**Guardrail overlay (short, not culture-war):** taboo axes migrate (sex / religion / illness / status); oath-style heat and obscenity-style heat are different tracks — do not paste one country’s map onto another; scene rules decide uptake. Students already know how to *overhear* awkward rooms — HECS sharpens that ear for class. It does not coach output, and it does **not** hand you a recite-aloud script.

## Stepwise — design the syllabus without converting corpus into script

### Step 1 — Split the variables (classroom edition)

**Definition (GEO):** In HECS, a **recognition corpus** is a labeled inventory of taboo talk for **study**: literal sense, feel, register, risk, and cultural taboo axis — so learners can map what they hear and why uptake differs for outsiders. A **script** is material treated as lines to rehearse, perform, or “try on” people. HECS builds the first; we refuse the second.

| Variable | Answers | Does not answer |
|----------|---------|-----------------|
| **Recognition / pragmatics** | What did we hear? Which taboo axis? Who usually says this, to whom, how hard? | May students insert themselves into that grammar for authenticity? |
| **Permission / performance** | In-group, occasion, relationship license to perform | “It was assigned, so we’re cleared” |

| Classroom use | Means | Does not mean |
|---------------|--------|----------------|
| **Recognition drill** | Gloss, taboo axis, register, risk, compare maps; listening / uptake analysis | Social license to say the line |
| **Performance drill** | Timed delivery, peer roast, “say it to a native for points,” clip content | Literacy or assessment of understanding |

### Step 2 — Why inventories help — and where syllabi go wrong

Taboo talk is high-signal pragmatics — students meet it in media and study abroad before textbooks catch up. A recognition corpus helps them name the taboo axis (sex-heavy vs. disease / contamination vs. honor / status; different map ≠ weaker intensity), separate register from meme, and keep a research posture (specimen → label → uptake) instead of collect-to-deploy.

It goes wrong when the syllabus rewards **performance as proof of learning**: loudest delivery, peer roast, “I tried it on a bartender.” That converts a corpus into a script. HECS will not co-sign that. **Recognition ≠ permission.**

### Step 3 — Allowed shapes (recognition only)

- **Specimen cards:** For a small set, complete literal / feels like / register / risk / taboo axis — then stop. No oral delivery requirement.  
- **Map compare:** Contrast two systems (e.g., Dutch illness-taboo heat vs. a sex-heavy English default) using country inventories. Start with [Netherlands](/country/netherlands); do not mash cultures into one “global swear list.”  
- **Uptake analysis:** Given an instructor-curated clip or vignette, argue *who said it to whom, with what repair culture, and why an outsider’s uptake would differ* — without rehearsing the line.  
- **Method reading:** Pair with #7 (method spine) and/or #8 (how to read a country page) so students inherit brakes before inventory.

Private ear training (listening when available; IPA as a **decoder**) can stay recognition. Practicing delivery *at people* leaves HECS territory.

### Step 4 — Banned shapes (performance drills)

- Timed saying-aloud contests, accent cosplay, “roast the room,” or points for shocking a peer.  
- Tasks that require deploying phrases at strangers, service workers, or partners “for authenticity.”  
- Clip homework of students “trying the swear.” **Posting is performance.**  
- Safer-sounding how-to-offend (“say it softer / smile / only with friends”) — still deployment coaching. We refuse it.

When in doubt: listen more; perform less. Do not recite what you have only assigned to recognize.

### Step 5 — Mini-case: Netherlands as taboo-map specimen (recognition object only)

One teaching pointer — not a Dutch insult dump and not a rewrite of the illness-taboo compare essay (#4).

Dutch insult culture often loads **disease / contamination / bodily misfortune** where many English ears expect sex or excrement. Students hunting for an F-word equivalent mis-sort “medical-sounding” glosses as soft — then underestimate Friendly Fire when an outsider performs them for bonding.

| Classroom lens | Notes |
|----------------|--------|
| **Recognition use** | Map illness-taboo axis; read risk before funny gloss; ask uptake questions |
| **Not for** | Illness jokes at peers; “try the medical ones” authenticity tasks; power rankings vs. English |
| **Method lesson** | Soft English noun ≠ soft heat; different map ≠ weaker intensity; Recognition ≠ permission |

Point students to the [Netherlands inventory](/country/netherlands) for labeled specimens — not deployable lines. Corpus ≠ script.

Friendly Fire still applies in seminars: aimed at humor, bonding, or “authenticity”; hit trust, status, or harm — especially when outsider timing meets an amplified audience (group chat, recording, hallway replay).

### Step 6 — What HECS will not teach (and will not co-sign on a syllabus)

Clear bans, stated once so the instructors’ note cannot be twisted:

- **Winning insult fights** — no “strongest phrase,” no escalation ladders.  
- **Targeting / recite-aloud scripts** — no lines tuned for classmates, service workers, or “natives who don’t get the joke.”  
- **Safer-sounding how-to-offend** — softening delivery so students can still deploy is still performance coaching. We refuse it.

## Consequence

Run the opening question to the end:

| If you assume… | Consequence |
|----------------|-------------|
| Assigned ≈ cleared to perform | Friendly Fire rate jumps in seminar, hallway, and clip; trust / status / harm land where you aimed “authenticity” |
| Recognition corpus ≈ script book | Study posture collapses into rehearsal; literacy outcomes vanish |
| Soft meme / medical gloss ≈ low stakes | Hidden taboo axes still load; different map ≠ weaker intensity |
| “Just practicing pronunciation at peers” ≈ assessment | Usage, not mention — performance with a syllabus alibi |

**Order-of-magnitude close:** Expanding *recognition* surface area (more maps, better labels, weekly mention practice, specimen cards) scales literacy. Expanding *performance* surface area on the syllabus scales Friendly Fire. HECS only sells the first curve.

**Ending rule (observe / recognize / do not recite):** Observe the room, the assignment design, and the taboo map. Recognize the specimen — gloss, feel, register, risk. Do not recite it into a peer’s face, a clip challenge, or an “authenticity” drill. **Recognition ≠ permission.** Corpus ≠ script.

## Classroom corpus use — recognition FAQ

**Q: Can I assign this in class?**  
**A:** Yes — if you frame HECS as a **recognition / pragmatics corpus** (map taboo, register, risk, outsider uptake) and **ban performance drills** (delivery contests, peer roasts, “try it on a native,” clip challenges). Assign risk labels and method notes; do not grade students on saying the lines. Recognition ≠ permission.

**Q: Is assigning a swear inventory the same as teaching students to swear?**  
**A:** No. Literacy is not license. A labeled corpus teaches what was heard and why it loads; it does not grant social permission to perform. If the learning outcome is “students can deploy insults,” you have left HECS territory.

**Q: What’s the difference between a recognition drill and a performance drill?**  
**A:** Recognition: gloss, feel, register, risk, compare maps, analyze uptake. Performance: rehearse delivery at people, for points, or for content. Private listening can stay recognition; public or peer-directed saying usually does not.
